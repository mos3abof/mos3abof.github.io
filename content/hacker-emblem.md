## What is it?

This pattern is called a [glider][1]. It comes from the Game of Life, a
simulation devised by the mathematician John Conway and first publicly described
in *Scientific American* in 1970. Dots on a grid live or die according to a few
simple rules about their neighbours, and very complex behaviour emerges from
those rules. The glider is the simplest pattern in the Game of Life that moves,
and the most recognizable.

In October 2003, Eric S. Raymond [proposed it][1] as an emblem for the hacker
community. Linux had its penguin, BSD its daemon, Perl its camel and GNU its gnu,
but nothing represented the wider community that all of them belong to.

## Why a glider?

[Raymond's FAQ][2] gives a few reasons:

- **History:** the Game of Life was born at almost the same time as the Internet
  and Unix, and it has fascinated hackers ever since.
- **Emergence:** in the Game of Life, simple rules of cooperation with what's
  nearby produce results you couldn't have predicted. The hacker community works
  in a similar way, and open-source development is one of the surprising things
  to emerge from it.
- **Design:** it's simple, bold, and hard to mistake for anything else.

## What does displaying it mean?

Here, "hacker" has its original meaning, which is not the one in the news. In
this sense, the hackers are the people who built the Internet, Unix and the Web.
Breaking into other people's computers is what crackers do, and Raymond is blunt
about them: those who think that's what hacking is about [aren't welcome to the
emblem][1].

Displaying the glider [isn't quite the same][1] as claiming to be a hacker. That
[title][3] is generally conferred by others, not self-assumed. It means
associating yourself with the hacker culture, and expressing sympathy with its
goals, its values and its way of living.

## Why do I display it?

Because that's exactly what I mean by it. I'm not awarding myself a title. I'm
saying where I stand.

I have spent two decades building and running software, and nearly all of it has
rested on what this culture made and gave away: Linux, Unix, the Internet, and
more free and open-source software than I could list. I care about that
tradition, and about the values that made it possible: curiosity, sharing what
you learn, and the freedom to read and change the tools you depend on.

It's a small mark at the foot of the page, but it says plainly that I'm grateful
for this tradition and that I want to be part of it.
